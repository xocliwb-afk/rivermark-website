## Know what you want to understand

Bring the questions you already have about the home's condition, maintenance, and unfamiliar systems. An inspection helps put visible conditions in context and identify concerns that need closer evaluation.

A home inspection is a visual, noninvasive evaluation of the contracted, visible, and safely accessible portions of the property on the inspection date. It is not a pass/fail exam, engineering certification, complete code audit, appraisal, repair estimate, warranty, or guarantee that every concealed or future condition will be found. Your signed agreement defines the service; the [Residential inspection overview](/services/residential-home-inspections/) explains its scope and limitations.

## Coordinate the visit before inspection day

Coordinate lawful access with the owner and the appropriate transaction participants. Confirm contingency dates and other contractual deadlines with your agent or attorney. An inspector does not interpret your contract or extend a legal deadline.

Before scheduling, gather the property address, type, approximate year, ownership form, total inspected area including the basement, number of units, additional buildings, and known access or utility limitations. Keep the client's and agent's details separate. If you do not know an answer, identify it as unknown so it can be reviewed.

For the steps from price check through report delivery, see [What Buyers Can Expect](/buyers/). Large, unusual, mixed-use, or access-restricted assignments may need individual review. Before arranging your day around a time, check the official appointment status and complete the required booking steps; beginning a quote alone does not confirm an appointment.

## Prepare the property for useful access

Ask the responsible party to arrange normal utilities and access before the visit. Preparation can include:

- Clear access to the electrical panel, heating and cooling equipment, water heater, and other contracted systems.
- Accessible attic and crawlspace entrances, keys, and permission for contracted outbuildings.
- Reasonable clearance around stored belongings and areas that need inspection.
- Safe arrangements for pets, occupants, gates, and secured areas.
- Disclosure of known shutdowns, leaks, hazards, and other limitations.

Do not operate equipment or move items unsafely to prepare for an inspection. An inspector cannot guarantee seller or occupant cooperation, and a component that cannot be inspected is not presumed satisfactory.

## Participate without interrupting safe work

Clients may attend all or part of the inspection where access permits. The inspector also needs uninterrupted time for safe observation, photographs, measurements, system operation, and notes.

The final 45–60 minutes can be a useful time to attend, depending on the property and the progress of the inspection. This is a planning estimate, not a fixed appointment segment. Remain reachable for timing updates. If you cannot attend, discuss an appropriate way to review the observations.

## Expect a property-specific field sequence

Weather, layout, access, occupancy, and safety affect the order of work. Exterior and drainage observations, roof conditions, structure, interior, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, insulation, ventilation, and other contracted components form part of the residential framework.

The inspector does not force unsafe roof access, open unsafe equipment, operate a questionable valve, or restart equipment that has been shut down simply to finish a checklist. Safety and actual conditions control what can be inspected.

## Understand and retain the limitations

Weather, stored belongings, locked rooms, unavailable utilities, unsafe conditions, and concealed construction can limit the inspection. Those limitations belong in the report because they affect what is known.

Access that becomes available later may reveal information that could not be observed during the original visit. A limitation is not a finding that the hidden component is in good condition. Where further evaluation or access is appropriate, the report should explain why.

## Use the walkthrough to prioritize

A walkthrough normally takes about 30–45 minutes, depending on the property and the questions. That is an estimate, not a guaranteed duration.

Expect discussion of the material observations, apparent urgent concerns, recommended specialist evaluation, near-term work, maintenance, and important limitations. Keep your questions handy:

- What did you observe that makes this important?
- What is known, and what remains uncertain?
- What type of professional should evaluate it, and why?
- What should I monitor if action is not immediate?

Onsite explanations are preliminary. After photographs, notes, and measurements are reviewed, the written report provides the inspection's written findings and recommendations. Use that reviewed account when it differs from the discussion; dated corrections or addenda may follow when needed.

## Read the written report in full

A useful finding explains what was observed, where it was observed, why it matters, and a reasonable next step. Supporting photographs provide context; the report is not a complete photographic inventory of every surface.

Read beyond the summary. Narratives, limitations, related observations, and maintenance guidance may change how you understand a finding. The number of findings is not a score for the home. The [report-reading guide](/resources/how-to-read-home-inspection-report/) explains how to weigh significance, uncertainty, and next steps.

The report does not provide repair bids, legal or negotiation advice, a purchase recommendation, or a guarantee about future performance. When another professional needs to evaluate a condition, allow that person to determine the appropriate diagnostic or repair scope.

## Keep client and agent roles clear

An agent may help coordinate access and timing while you remain the inspection client. Identify the client, who accepts the agreement, who pays, and who is authorized to receive the report separately.

Report sharing requires your authorization and follows the agreement. Confirm the intended recipients with Rivermark, especially when a deadline depends on delivery; do not assume a portal-sharing arrangement is available.

## Ask useful follow-up questions

Rivermark's policy includes reasonable report-related email clarification for approximately 30 calendar days after the inspection for the original client. This is clarification of the report and observed conditions, not an unlimited consulting engagement.

It does not include another site visit, contractor management, bid review, renovation design, legal or engineering advice, or diagnosis of a new condition from a distance. The agreement and any separately accepted service govern additional work.

Useful questions include which observation supports a conclusion, why a specialist is recommended, what limitation remains unresolved, and how a maintenance item differs from a near-term concern. Decisions about the purchase, contract, financing, or negotiation remain with you and your advisers.
